Basics — two labels, one clip

The whole capability in one interaction: play a voice into the checkpoint and read its two acoustic labels, their probabilities, and — most importantly — how close the call was. A confident acoustic read and a coin-flip look identical if you only show the winning label; the margin is what keeps you honest.

Framing (same as the main page)

“female” / “male” here are binary acoustic voice labels from LibriSpeech training data — a description of how a clip sounds to the model, never a statement about a person's gender or identity. The binary label set erases non-binary people and the training data skews by accent, age and recording conditions. Never use this output for identity, employment, access, safety, or any other consequential decision about a person.

Run it

Pick a bundled sample (all rights-safe, attributed below), drop a file, or record up to 10 s of your own voice. First use downloads ~319 MB with real progress; after that the cached model starts automatically.

The abstention threshold is your review bar: when the winning margin (|p₁ − p₂|) is below it, the page refuses to name a label and says “ask a human” instead. Set it to 100% to see the abstain state on any clip.

What to notice

  • Both labels always show. A binary classifier's answer is only meaningful next to the runner-up — 99.9% / 0.1% and 51% / 49% are very different statements.
  • The margin is the honest signal. Confident clips have margins near 100%; anything low should go to a human, which is exactly what the threshold encodes.
  • The label follows the sound, not the speaker. These four samples come from different languages and readers; the model still assigns acoustic labels from pitch/timbre. Try your own voice at different pitches and watch it move.
  • Non-English speech still gets labelled. The checkpoint was trained on English audiobooks; out-of-distribution clips (Polish, Italian, Spanish, Mandarin) get whatever acoustic read its training says — a bias to remember, not a fact about the reader.
